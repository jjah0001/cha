What is tea?

一叶六色

Every true tea comes from one plant, Camellia sinensis. What happens to the leaf after picking decides which of six colours it becomes.

Chinese tea is sorted into six types, named for the colour of the leaf or the brewed liquor. Tap a colour on the wheel to see how it's made, what it tastes like and which teas to try first.

One plantGreen, white, oolong, black and Pu'er all come from Camellia sinensis. Herbal "teas" like chamomile are infusions, not tea.
Oxidation is the dialOnce a leaf is bruised it browns, like a cut apple. Stopping that early keeps tea green; letting it run makes it red.
Red, not blackWhat English calls black tea, China calls red tea (红茶) for its liquor. China's "black" tea (黑茶) is dark, aged tea like Pu'er.

What are you eating?

茶配

Name a dish, any cuisine, and get three teas from the atlas that suit it, with the reason for each.

Match weightDelicate food, delicate tea. Light greens and whites with seafood and salads; dark and roasted teas with red meat and rich sauces.
Cut the fatRoasted oolongs and Pu'er cleanse the palate after fried, fatty or creamy food.
Echo a flavourSmoky with smoky, fruity with fruit desserts, nutty greens with nuts and grains.
Calm the heatWith chilli, pick a smooth, low-bitterness tea and brew it a little cooler.

Sources and notes

Sources checked. History and brewing details were cross-checked against Chinese-language sources, including China's national intangible heritage register (中国非物质文化遗产网), Xinhua, The Paper (澎湃新闻), People's Daily, Lugu Township Office (Taiwan) and Taiwan's Tea Research and Extension Station reporting. Where legends differ, the page says so. Entries whose specific history no Chinese source confirmed are marked "General entry" on their card and kept to basics. Food pairings are suggestions, not sourced facts.

Listed teas. entries come from published registers and lists. These include regional teas, historic bushes and tea-growing places, not a count of botanically distinct varieties. English names are pinyin transliterations.

Evidence and suggestions. Newly researched facts link to their Chinese-language sources beside each field. Missing facts are marked as unrecorded. Food pairings are editorial suggestions. Timer settings are adjustable starting points, not sourced recipes for every named tea. Existing profile prose and radar scores have not all been re-audited.
