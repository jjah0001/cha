What is tea?

一叶六色

Every true tea comes from one plant, Camellia sinensis. What happens to the leaf after picking decides which of six colours it becomes.

Chinese tea is sorted into six types, named for the colour of the leaf or the brewed liquor. Tap a colour on the wheel to see how it's made, what it tastes like and which teas to try first.

One plantGreen, white, oolong, black and Pu'er all come from Camellia sinensis. Herbal "teas" like chamomile are infusions, not tea.
Oxidation is the dialOnce a leaf is bruised it browns, like a cut apple. Stopping that early keeps tea green; letting it run makes it red.
Red, not blackWhat English calls black tea, China calls red tea (红茶) for its liquor. China's "black" tea (黑茶) is post-fermented dark tea, such as ripe Pu'er, Anhua bricks and Liu Bao.

What are you eating?

茶配

Name a dish, any cuisine, and get three teas from the atlas that suit it, with the reason for each.

Match weightDelicate food, delicate tea. Light greens and whites with seafood and salads; dark and roasted teas with red meat and rich sauces.
Cut the fatRoasted oolongs and Pu'er cleanse the palate after fried, fatty or creamy food.
Echo a flavourSmoky with smoky, fruity with fruit desserts, nutty greens with nuts and grains.
Calm the heatWith chilli, pick a smooth, low-bitterness tea and brew it a little cooler.

About Cha

关于我们

Cha is a guide to the teas of mainland China and Taiwan: where they grow, how they taste, how to brew them, what to eat with them, and which one suits you. You don’t need to know any Chinese to use it.

What Cha is for

The name is a small pun. In Chinese, the word for tea, 茶 chá, sounds exactly like the word for “to look something up”, 查 chá. Cha brings together more than two thousand teas, from world-famous ones such as Dragon Well green tea 西湖龙井 Xīhú Lóngjǐng and Big Red Robe oolong 大红袍 Dà Hóng Páo to small local teas that few people outside their home county have heard of, so you can find one, learn about it, and brew it well.

  • Only real teas. Every tea here is confirmed by published sources, listed under “Sources and notes” at the foot of each page. Nothing is invented to fill a gap.
  • For everyone. Everything is explained in English, with the Chinese names alongside for when you shop or order. Chinese is written in two styles: simplified characters, used in mainland China, and traditional characters, used in Taiwan and Hong Kong. The 简/繁 switch at the top changes between them.
  • Yours to keep. Your shelf of teas stays in your own browser. Anyone can give a tea a star rating, without signing up.
  • A tea master to talk to. In the Tea house, Master Lin (林师傅), an AI tea master from the Wuyi Mountains, answers your questions about tea in your own language, or simply keeps you company over a cup and asks about where you come from.
  • A tea for today. Each day Cha suggests a tea that suits your season, and everyone in your half of the world gets the same one.

Tea with Master Lin

In the Tea house you take a seat at the counter of a small family tea shop beside the Nine-Bend Stream. Its owner is Master Lin 林师傅 Lín shīfu: Lin is his family name, and shīfu is the respectful word Chinese people use for a master of a craft. He has made rock tea here for decades, and as a young man he travelled through China’s tea regions, so he knows teas of all six colours from every province and from Taiwan, not only the ones from his own mountain.

  • Ask him about tea. What to try first, how to brew it, where a tea comes from, or what a Chinese word on the packet means.
  • Or just chat. He likes company and is curious about where you come from: now and then he’ll ask about your home, what people drink there and what season it is for you.
  • In your language. He answers in whatever language you write in, and explains Chinese words as he goes.
  • He knows what’s outside his window. The season, the weather, the time of day and any festival, the same as the mountain behind the page.

Master Lin is a made-up character played by an AI. He can be wrong, so check anything important, especially about health, and he never sells anything. Your conversation isn’t saved: when you leave the page, it’s gone. To answer you, the page sends your messages, the country your device’s time zone points to, and the scene on the mountain to an AI service (Anthropic’s Claude) through Cha’s own server.

The mountain behind the page

Behind every page is a tea mountain in the Wuyi Mountains 武夷山 Wǔyí Shān of Fujian province, in south-east China, a UNESCO World Heritage Site and the home of “rock tea” 岩茶 yánchá, oolongs grown among the cliffs. You can make out rust-red sandstone peaks, small stone-walled terraces of tea bushes tucked in among them, the family tea house where the leaves are processed, and the Nine-Bend Stream 九曲溪 Jiǔqū Xī winding below, where visitors float down on bamboo rafts.

The scene is drawn faintly so it never gets in the way, and it is alive. It follows your season, your sunrise and sunset, and the tea grower’s year as it is lived on the mountain.

Through the year

The traditional Chinese calendar divides the year into twenty-four “solar terms” 节气 jiéqì of about fifteen days each, named after the weather and the work on the land: “Insects Awaken” in early March, “Clear and Bright” in early April, “Frost’s Descent” in late October. Tea growers still plan their year by them, so the mountain does too.

Festivals and tea days

Most Chinese festivals follow the old lunar calendar, in which each month begins at a new moon. That is why their dates move around from year to year in the Western calendar.

FestivalWhat it is, and what happens on the mountain
Chinese New Year
春节 Chūnjié		The biggest festival of the year, also called the Spring Festival, when families gather. Red lanterns go up and red couplets 春联 chūnlián are pasted round the door. Firecrackers and fireworks scare off bad luck, most of all on New Year’s Eve 除夕 chúxī, when the whole family sits down together to the reunion dinner 年夜饭 niányèfàn; you can hear the talk, the laughter and the toasts from the tea house. In the first days a lion dance performs by day while a crowd gathers round: two dancers inside one lion costume, the front one working the big head with its mirror and horn, blinking, snapping its jaw and shaking its head to drums and gongs, and now and then rearing up as the front dancer is lifted high.New Year’s Eve Lion dance
Lantern Festival
元宵 Yuánxiāo		The fifteenth night of the new year, under the first full moon, which closes the New Year season. Lanterns line the path and a glowing dragon lantern, carried on poles by a team of people, winds along it, chasing a shining pearl held up on a pole in front.See it
Calling the Mountain
喊山 hǎnshān		An old Wuyi custom held on “Insects Awaken” day. Growers gather at the old imperial tea garden with red flags, drums and gongs to wake the tea bushes for the new season. Then everyone shouts together, “tea, sprout!”, 茶发芽 chá fāyá.See it
Tomb-Sweeping Day
清明 Qīngmíng		“Clear and Bright”, when families climb the hill to sweep and tidy their ancestors’ graves, leave food and burn paper money for them, and set off a string of firecrackers before going home. In the hills round Wuyi it usually comes in a fine drizzle, just as the first tea is picked.See it
Tea contest
斗茶赛 dòuchá sài		Once the spring tea is made, growers bring their best to be tasted side by side, and the winner is crowned “tea king” 茶王 cháwáng. People crowd round the tea house, and there is a cheer for the winner.See it
Dragon Boat Festival
端午 Duānwǔ		An early-summer festival. Bunches of mugwort and calamus are hung by the door to keep illness away, and families eat zongzi 粽子 zòngzi, sticky rice wrapped in leaves. Long, narrow boats with dragon heads race to the beat of a drum, and on the mountain two of them race down the Nine-Bend Stream.See it
Ghost Festival
中元 Zhōngyuán		A quiet evening for remembering ancestors and the dead. The temple bell sounds at dusk, and small lanterns are floated down the stream.See it
Mid-Autumn Festival
中秋 Zhōngqiū		The harvest full moon, the roundest and brightest of the year and a symbol of family reunion. Families come home for a big dinner together, then sit outside with tea and mooncakes to admire the moon while the children run about with lanterns. On the mountain the tea house is lit up and full of talk and laughter, children with lanterns run along the path, a few firecrackers go off in the villages, and the clouds part for a large, glowing moon.See it
Double Ninth
重阳 Chóngyáng		The ninth day of the ninth month, a day for climbing to high places and honouring elders. Climbers appear on the peaks and chrysanthemums by the door.See it
Tea Expo
茶博会 chábóhuì		A large tea fair held in the town of Wuyishan every November. Visitors walk up the path to the tea house.See it
Laba
腊八 Làbā		The eighth day of the last lunar month. Families cook a porridge of grains, beans and nuts, and temples give it out, so the pot goes on at first light.See it
Winter Solstice
冬至 Dōngzhì		The longest night of the year, a family festival in its own right. On the eve, the family rolls sweet rice balls 汤圆 tāngyuán at the table to eat next morning.See it

North and south

The seasons on the mountain follow where you are. The lunar festivals keep their real dates everywhere, and the Winter Solstice falls on your own shortest day.

The sound

Every sound you hear is made live in your browser, from scratch. Rain, water, wind, fire, insects, birds, bells and drums are all built from simple tones and noise, with no recordings, so the page stays light and the mountain never plays the same minute twice. The sound starts with the page; the speaker button beside the season label at the top turns it off, and tapping the season label stops the animation. If your device asks for less motion, the scene moves more gently.

Sources and notes

Sources checked. History and brewing details were cross-checked against Chinese-language sources, including China's national intangible heritage register (中国非物质文化遗产网), Xinhua, The Paper (澎湃新闻), People's Daily, Lugu Township Office (Taiwan) and Taiwan's Tea Research and Extension Station reporting. Where legends differ, the page says so. Entries whose specific history no Chinese source confirmed are marked "General entry" on their card and kept to basics. Food pairings are suggestions, not sourced facts.

Listed teas. register-based profiles come from published registers and lists. These include regional teas, historic bushes and tea-growing places, not a count of botanically distinct varieties. English names are pinyin transliterations.

Evidence and suggestions. Newly researched facts link to their Chinese-language sources beside each field. Missing facts are marked as unrecorded. Food pairings are editorial suggestions. Timer settings are adjustable starting points, not sourced recipes for every named tea. Existing profile prose and radar scores have not all been re-audited.
